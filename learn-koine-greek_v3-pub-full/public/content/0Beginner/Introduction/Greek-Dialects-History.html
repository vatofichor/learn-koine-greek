Overview: Greek Dialect Over Time

The development of Ancient Greek is a history of tribal migration and regional isolation leading to distinct
linguistic "colors."

These dialects eventually coalesced into Koine (Common) Greek, the language of the Septuagint (LXX) and the New
Testament.

The Linguistic Branches

  • Attic: The dialect of Athens. It is the most prestigious literary form, used by Thucydides, Plato, and
Xenophon. It is the primary ancestor of Koine Greek.
  • Doric (Dorian): Spoken in the Peloponnese (Sparta), Crete, and Rhodes. It is characterized by its conservatism
and the retention of the long "a" (α) where Attic uses "e" (η).
  • Aeolic (Aeolian): Spoken in Boeotia, Thessaly, and Lesbos (notably by Sappho). It retained archaic features
and unique accentuation patterns.
  • Ionic: Closely related to Attic, spoken on the Ionian coast (Asia Minor). It is the language of Homeric epic
and Herodotus.
  • Archaic: Not a specific dialect, but the period (c. 800–480 BC) during which the Greek alphabet was
standardized from Phoenician scripts and the dialects began to be recorded.
  • Phoenician: A non-Greek, Semitic influence. The Phoenicians provided the phonetic alphabet which the Greeks
adapted by adding vowels, transforming it into a true alphabetic system.

Context Relative to the Septuagint (LXX)

The Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible (3rd–2nd century BC), represents a pivot point in
linguistic history.

  • Levelling of Dialects: By the time of the LXX, the distinct features of Doric, Aeolic, and Ionic had largely
merged into Attic-based Koine.
  • Semitic Interference: While the grammar is Greek, the LXX contains Hebraisms (syntax and idioms carried over
from the Hebrew source).
  • Expansion of Meaning: Greek philosophical terms (Attic/Ionic roots) were repurposed to express Hebrew
theological concepts (e.g., doxa moving from "opinion" to "glory").
  • The Phoenician Link: The LXX represents the "return" of the alphabet to a Semitic context. The Greek script
(derived from Phoenician) was used to translate the records of the Hebrews (neighbors to the Phoenicians).

Additional Groups Relevant to the Septuagint

  • Macedonians: Their conquest under Alexander spread the Greek language, creating the necessity for the LXX as
Jews in the diaspora lost fluency in Hebrew.
  • Alexandrians: The specific melting-pot culture of Egypt where the translation occurred, influencing the
"Alexandrian" text-type.
  • Hellenistic Jews: The primary "bridge" people who functioned in a Semitic thought-world but a Greek
linguistic-world.

The Linguistic "Bigger Picture"

To understand the connectedness of these groups to the Septuagint, visualize the process as a funnel:

  • Input (Archaic Period): Distinct tribes (Dorian, Ionian, Aeolian) maintained isolated dialects. Writing was
standardized via the Phoenician alphabet.
  • Compression (Classical/Hellenistic): The rise of Athens (Attic) and later the Macedonian Empire forced these
dialects to merge for trade and administration. This resulted in Koine (Common Greek).
  • Output (The LXX): Koine was utilized by Alexandrian scholars and Hellenistic Jews to translate Hebrew
concepts. This created a unique linguistic hybrid: Greek words (morphology) functioning with Hebrew logic (syntax).

Geographic/Tribal Distribution

  • Attic/Ionic: The "Intellectual" layer.
  • Doric/Aeolic: The "Regional" layer (largely absorbed by the 3rd century BC).
  • Phoenician: The "Technological" layer (the script itself).
  • Macedonian/Alexandrian: The "Globalist" layer (the spread of the language).

Critical Analysis of the Linguistic Pivot

The transition from specific tribal dialects to the language of the Septuagint is not merely a "simplification."
It is a functional shift.
While the Classical dialects (Doric, Aeolic) were tied to local identity and ritual, Koine Greek (the language of
the LXX) became a "logic-based system" designed for universal communication across the Mediterranean.
