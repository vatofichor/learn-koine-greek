Critical Data Points

  • Linguistic Shift: Facilitated the transition of Semitic theological concepts into the Greek philosophical lexicon
(e.g., translating Torah as Nomos).
  • Textual Divergence: The LXX frequently follows a "Vorlage" (source text) that differs from the Masoretic Text (MT).
For example, the LXX version of Jeremiah is approximately 1/8th shorter and arranged differently than the MT.
  • New Testament Integration: Roughly 75% to 90% of Old Testament citations found in the New Testament are quoted
directly from the LXX rather than the Hebrew.
  • The "Seventy" (LXX): Derived from the Letter of Aristeas, which claims 72 Jewish scholars translated the Pentateuch
in 72 days in Alexandria. Systemic Connectedness

The Septuagint (LXX) is the Koine Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, traditionally dated to the 3rd and 2nd
centuries BCE. It served as the primary scriptural text for the Hellenistic Jewish diaspora and the early Christian
Church.

Developmental Identifiers

  • The "Pentateuch": Initially, only the first five books of Moses were referred to as the "Translation of the
Seventy." It was only later that the entire Greek corpus of Jewish scripture (including the Prophets and Writings) was subsumed under this single title.
  • The Law (Ho Nomos): In the Hellenistic Jewish diaspora (Alexandria), the Greek translation was often simply called
Ho Nomos (The Law), as it functioned as the official civic and religious law for Greek-speaking Jews.
  • The Seventy-Two: In the earliest account (Letter of Aristeas), the number of translators was actually 72 (six from
each of the twelve tribes). The name was rounded down to "Seventy" for symbolic and practical ease.
  • The "Christian" Bible: After the Jewish community largely moved toward the Masoretic Text (Hebrew) in the 2nd
century CE, the Septuagint was sometimes pejoratively or descriptively called the "Christian Old Testament" by Jewish polemicists of that era.

Logic of the Name

The name is not an internal title found in the text itself, but a provenance marker. It asserts the authority of the translation by linking it to the legendary 70 elders of Israel, echoing the 70 elders who ascended Mount Sinai with Moses (Exodus 24:9).

Critical Data Points

  • The Twelve: In the LXX, the "Minor Prophets" are often placed before the Major Prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel).
  • Numeration: The Greek titles for the historical books (Βασιλειῶν Α'-Δ') emphasize a unified four-part history of
kingship rather than the Hebrew division of Samuel and Kings.
  • Spelling: Notice the Greek shift in phonetics (e.g., Hosea becomes Ὡσηέ, Habakkuk becomes Ἀμβακούμ).

The "Vowel" Problem

Hebrew was originally written without vowels. Meaning was derived from the three-consonant root (TRG).
  • Systemic Risk: A single set of consonants could mean "King" (MeLeK) or "Angel" (MaL'aK) depending on oral tradition.
  • LXX Role: By translating into Greek (which has fixed vowels), the LXX "locked in" specific interpretations 1,000
years before the Hebrew MT was standardized with vowel points.

2. Decoding YHWH (The Tetragrammaton)

You will encounter the term YHWH (the "four letters"). In Hebrew, this is the specific, non-generic name of the deity.

The Linguistic Pivot: Kyrios

  • The Hebrew Logic: The name was considered too sacred to pronounce. Readers would see YHWH but say Adonai (Lord).
  • The Greek Logic: The LXX translators didn't transliterate the name (they didn't write "Iave"). They translated the
substitution.
  • The Result: Everywhere the Hebrew says YHWH, the Greek says Kyrios (Κύριος - Lord).
  • Critical Shift: This stripped the deity of a "personal name" and replaced it with a "title of authority," aligning
better with Greek political and philosophical hierarchies.

3. Semantic Mapping: From Concrete to Abstract

Greek is a language of "being"; Hebrew is a language of "doing."
When you learn Greek, notice these shifts in the LXX:

Case Study: Torah vs. Nomos

  • Hebrew (Torah): Means "instruction," "path," or "teaching." It is a dynamic process.
  • Greek (Nomos): Means "law," "statute," or "custom." It implies a rigid, codified legal system.
  • Systemic Impact: The LXX turned a "way of life" into a "legal code," which heavily influenced Western jurisprudence
and Roman law.

Case Study: Nephesh vs. Psyche

  • Hebrew (Nephesh): A "living throat" or "breathing creature." It is holistic (body + breath).
  • Greek (Psyche): In the Greek mind, this carried Platonic baggage of a "soul" distinct from the body.
  • Systemic Impact: The LXX introduced a dualism (Body vs. Soul) into the text that was largely absent in the original
Hebrew thought-structure.

4. Phonetic "Greeknification"

As a Greek learner, you’ll notice names look "off." This is due to the lack of certain sounds in Greek (like "sh" or "ch").
  • The Terminal 'S': Greek requires most masculine nouns to end in a nominative marker (usually -s).
  • Yeshua (Hebrew) → Iesous (Greek)
  • Moshe (Hebrew) → Moyses (Greek)
  • The Gutteral Drop: Greek lacks the "Ayin" (a deep throat sound).
  • Gaza (Greek) is actually ‘Azzah in Hebrew. The "G" was the closest the Greeks could get to the vibrating throat
sound.

5. Logical Flow of the LXX vs. MT

The MT (Hebrew) ends with Chronicles, focusing on the "Return to the Land." The LXX ends with The Prophets (Malachi), focusing on a "Future Expectation."

Why this matters to you:

View the LXX not just as a translation, but as a re-architecting of information. It took a tribal, Semitic collection of scrolls and formatted them into a "Library" (the Biblia) compatible with the Library of Alexandria’s standards.

Logic Shift: The Tetragrammaton (YHWH)

In the Hebrew system, YHWH is a verbal derivative of "to be" (Hayah), implying a dynamic existence ("He who causes to be"). When the LXX translated this as Ho Ōn (Ὁ Ὤν), it shifted the logic into the realm of Greek philosophy.
  • Hebrew Logic: God is the one who acts in history.
  • Greek Logic: God is the "Supreme Being" or "The One Who Is" (Pure Existence).

The Linguistic "Interface"

When you analyze these shifts, think of it as a software port.
  • Hebrew (Source Code): Highly contextual, dependent on oral tradition (vowels), and visually compact.
  • Greek (Target Environment): Explicit, vowel-heavy, and designed for logic, rhetoric, and public debate.

Key Data Point: The "Hapax Legomena"

The LXX contains many words that appear only once in the entire corpus. Translators often had to invent Greek words or repurpose obscure ones to capture Semitic nuances. This created a specific "Dialect" often called Jewish-Greek, which is distinct from the Attic Greek used by philosophers like Plato.

The Septuagint's Reach

The LXX did not just translate words; it re-organized the "Big Picture" of Western thought.
  • Standardization: It provided a uniform text for the Mediterranean world.
  • Education: It allowed non-Hebrew speakers (like yourself) to access Semitic theology through the "API" of the Greek
language.
  • Architecture: It established the book order (Pentateuch → History → Wisdom → Prophets) that most modern Bibles still
use today.
