Septuaginta (Formalization)

  • Definition: Latin title popularized by Augustine of Hippo (4th c. CE), referencing the "70" translators.

Augustine of Hippo

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) was a staunch defender of the Septuagint (LXX), viewing it as divinely inspired, not just a translation. Despite Jerome favoring Hebrew, Augustine prioritized the LXX for its authority in Western tradition and unity, arguing that disparities were prophetic enhancements. In his correspondence with Jerome, he feared that deviating from the LXX would damage the Church’s authority. He upheld the "Septuaginta auctoritas" (authority of the seventy), and his canonization of the OT included deuterocanonical books found in the Greek version. His approach combined theological belief in its inspiration with practical use in pastoral teaching.

The Masoretic Text

  • Definition: The authoritative Hebrew and Aramaic text of the 24 books of the Hebrew Bible in Rabbinic Judaism.

Lucianic

  • Definition: Lucianic refers to the style of 2nd-century Greek satirist Lucian of Samosata (witty, ironical) or, in theology, the 3rd/4th-century Lucian of Antioch's revision of the Greek Bible. It characterizes a smoother, more readable text, forming the basis for the Byzantine text-type and Textus Receptus.

The Lucianic Text (Antiochian)

A revision of the Septuagint (Greek Old Testament) by Lucian of Antioch, noted for improving Greek style, removing obscurities, and creating a smoother, more readable text.

Notes

Lucian of Antioch (c. 240–312) was an influential Christian theologian, presbyter, and martyr known for producing a critical, widely used revision of the Greek Bible (Septuagint/New Testament). As a key figure in the School of Antioch, he emphasized literal-historical interpretation over allegorical methods. His teachings on Christology are considered a forerunner to Arianism, as he taught that the Word was a created being. He died in Nicomedia during the persecution of Maximinus Daia.
  • Bible Revision: Created the "Lucianic" text, which influenced the Byzantine text-type.
  • Theology: Championed literal biblical interpretation, opposing Alexandrian allegory.
  • Legacy: Regarded as a precursor to Arius; his students included key Arian leaders.
  • Martyrdom: Executed in 312 AD, honored as a saint.

Theodotion

  • Definition: Theodotion (c. 180–190 CE) was a 2nd-century Hellenistic Jewish scholar known for producing a Greek translation of the Old Testament that revised the Septuagint to align closer to the Hebrew text.

Hellenistic

  • Definition: The word "Hellenistic" literally means to "imitate Greeks", thus the Hellenistic period was the time when Greek culture and Near East customs were combined. The era began in 323 B.C.E. when Alexander the Great existed and ended in 30 B.C.E. through the conquest of Rome in Egypt.

Semitic

  • Definition: Semitic refers to a diverse group of peoples—including Arabs, Hebrews, Assyrians, and Amharic speakers—originating in the ancient Near East and North Africa. They are primarily defined by speaking Afro-Asiatic languages like Arabic, Hebrew, and Aramaic. Their shared heritage, rooted in the Biblical name Shem, has shaped major Abrahamic religions.
